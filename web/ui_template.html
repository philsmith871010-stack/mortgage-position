Plain-English guide

What is a "basis point"?

A hundredth of a percentage point. 25bp is 0.25%. 100bp is one whole percent. Rates people talk about "moving 40bp" moved by 0.4%.

Where does a fixed mortgage rate actually come from?

Your lender borrows the money it lends you. To offer a fixed rate for two years it locks in its own cost of money for two years, using a market called the swap market. Then it adds a markup to cover its costs, the capital it must hold, and profit.

Fixed mortgage rate = swap rate + lender's markup. The swap rate is published every day by the Bank of England. Right now the two-year swap is about 4.2% and the average two-year mortgage is about 4.9%, so the markup is about 0.7%.

What do you mean by "what the market prices"?

If you know what it costs to lock in money for two years, and what it costs to lock in for four years, you can work out what the market is implying for the two years in between. That's arithmetic, not opinion. It's called the forward rate.

So when this page needs to know "what will a two-year fix cost in two years' time", it uses that forward rate plus the usual lender markup. It's the market's own price for the future — the same number a bank would use. It is not a forecast, and it can be wrong. The break-even tells you by how much it would need to be wrong to change your answer.

What is "present value" and why does it matter?

£100 in five years' time is worth less than £100 today, because you could use £100 today to pay down your mortgage and save interest on it. So before adding up payments made in different years, we shrink the later ones to what they're worth today. We shrink them at your mortgage rate — that's the return you'd get from paying the mortgage down instead.

Without this, a route that pushes cost into the future would look artificially cheap.

Why include "the balance still owed at the end"?

Different routes pay off the loan at different speeds. A lower rate pays off more of the balance each month. If we only counted payments, a route could look cheap simply by leaving you owing more at the end. Including what's still owed makes the comparison fair.

What is the free "relock" option?

Most lenders let you arrange your next deal up to six months before your current one ends. If a cheaper deal appears before your new one starts, you can switch to it instead. Since the 2023 Mortgage Charter this is standard practice at the big lenders.

That means you never lose by locking early. On Bank of England data since 2009, doing this saved about 0.25% on the rate on average, and saved something four times out of five. We credit 0.20% (after allowing for a bit of friction) — but only on deals you haven't taken yet, never on one you already hold. On a £250,000 five-year fix that's worth about £2,000, more than the typical fee.

What is the lender's markup, and why 62bp?

The gap between the swap rate and the mortgage rate. It's been roughly half a percent since 2022. Ten years ago it was over three percent — lenders have been competing much harder. We use the average of the last twelve months, and you can change it under Market inputs.

It matters less than you'd think. Since 2022, about nine-tenths of the movement in mortgage rates has been the swap rate moving, not the markup.

What is an early repayment charge?

A penalty for leaving a fixed deal early: a percentage of your balance, usually stepping down each year (for example 5%, 4%, 3%, 2%, 1%). Unlike the break costs banks charge each other, it doesn't change with interest rates — so when rates fall a lot, leaving can be cheap relative to the saving. This page works out whether it is.

Why does "fee added to the loan" matter?

Most people add the product fee to the mortgage rather than paying it upfront. It's convenient, but you then pay interest on it for the rest of the term — a £999 fee costs more than £999. The page includes that. Untick the box to see the difference.

What's the standard variable rate?

What your lender moves you onto if you do nothing when your deal ends. It's usually far higher than any fix on offer. The "if you do nothing" figure shows what inertia costs.

What this page does not do

It doesn't predict interest rates. If you believe rates will fall further than the market expects, the break-even tells you how much further they'd need to fall for the shorter fix to win.

It doesn't know your circumstances. If you might move house, want to overpay heavily, or need flexibility, a shorter fix buys that flexibility, and that isn't priced here.

It uses average rates at 75% loan-to-value. Your actual offer may be higher or lower; enter the rates you've actually been given.

It's a comparison of your own options against today's prices. It isn't advice, and it isn't a recommendation.
